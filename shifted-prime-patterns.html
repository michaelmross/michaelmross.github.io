How dense can a set be while avoiding shifted-prime patterns?

Certified lower-bound constructions for polynomial progressions with shifted-prime difference.

a, a + P1(p−1), …, a + Pk−1(p−1), p prime

At a glance. A theorem of Wooley and Ziegler says that every positive-density set contains shifted-prime polynomial progressions for infinitely many primes. Here we ask the complementary computational question: how dense can an explicit set remain while avoiding every such pattern up to a prime threshold X?

For the two-point pattern x, x + (p−1)², the best certified periodic constructions found here track roughly X−1/2 through most of the tested range, before an apparent steepening beyond X ≈ 10⁵. For x, x + (p−1), x + (p−1)², avoidance is substantially easier: the initial decay is close to X−0.267.

§1The question

A shifted prime pattern is a polynomial progression whose shift parameter is one less than a prime, with the polynomials Pi ∈ ℤ[y] having zero constant term. Wooley and Ziegler said these patterns are unavoidable: every set of positive density contains one for infinitely many primes p. The recent paper of Krause, Mousavi, Tao, and Teräväinen makes that quantitative from above, with density bounds that engage only at astronomical scales.

These notes ask the other question: Given a threshold X, how dense can a set be while avoiding the pattern for every prime p ≤ X?

The aim is not to settle the asymptotic problem, but to construct explicit lower bounds at certifiable scales, measure how their density decays, and identify the arithmetic structure of the best examples. Every construction and absence reported below is independently reproducible from a certificate.

§2The two-point campaign

Begin with the two-point pattern x, x + (p−1)². Blocking every prime p ≤ X means that the difference set of A avoids SX = {(p−1)² : p ≤ X} — an instance of Motzkin's difference-avoidance problem.

A useful periodic family is given by Cantor–Gordon interval sets {n : cn mod m < L}. Exact circulant independence numbers can sometimes do better. The best certified constructions found so far are:

The main scaling.

Concretely, the X = 10⁵ record means that, for every window N up to 10¹⁰, there are explicit sets of density 21/5185 ≈ 0.004 containing no pattern x, x + (p−1)² at all — a regime where the known upper bounds say nothing yet. Across four decades the records track X−1/2 closely, with fitted exponent 0.50. Under N = X², that becomes N−1/4, just above the N−0.267 shape of Ruzsa's square-difference constructions.

The apparent bend.

The normalized quantity δ₂(X)√X stays in the band [1.26, 2.18] through X = 10⁵, then falls to 0.94 at 3·10⁵ and 0.84 at 10⁶. The top-of-ladder local slopes are near 0.6, so the profile appears to steepen beyond X ≈ 10⁵.

This is not simply a consequence of stopping close to the winning modulus. At X = 3·10⁵, the search reached about 9.7 times the winning modulus. At X = 10⁶, a 94-hour blind sweep of 150001 < m ≤ 400000 found nothing better; its maximum merely reproduced the record at a doubled modulus. The bend therefore survives blind coverage to about 3.6 times the winning modulus.

A conductor pattern.

The record also changes shape at X = 10⁶: m jumps tenfold and L jumps to 92. Yet a striking arithmetic regularity persists. After removing powers of two, every prime factor seen in a winning conductor is congruent to 1 mod 4: examples include 5·29, 2³·13·37, 5·17·37, and 5·17·61. At X = 10⁶, 109922 = 2·17·53·61. This ten-for-ten regularity is empirical; explaining it remains open.

§3A calibration that bit back

The first structured set we tried looked like a discovery. Take base-8 integers whose digits all lie in {0, 1, 3} and search for square differences (P(y) = y²): 435 of the 550 primes up to 4000 admit no pattern.

That is not resistance. Reduced mod 8, the set is {0, 1, 3}; no two of these residues differ by 4. But (p−1)² ≡ 4 (mod 8) exactly when p ≡ 3 (mod 4). An entire residue class of primes was therefore blocked before any detailed search began.

The harness now performs this diagnosis automatically: a residue scan reduces A modulo small moduli and identifies no-pattern verdicts explained by congruence alone. The methodological lesson is simple: read the residue scan before treating a large collection of null results as evidence of global structure.

§4Anatomy of a record

The X = 10⁴ winner is the set {n : 55n mod 3848 < 52}. Its proof is geometric: multiply every forbidden shift (p−1)² by 55 and reduce modulo 3848. The 1,229 primes up to 10⁴ produce 263 distinct residue classes, and none comes within 52 of zero. The resulting wedge is empty, so no difference of two set elements can equal a shifted-prime square.

For a construction (m, c, L), each prime has margin |c(p−1)²|m ≥ L, and L is the minimum of these margins over p ≤ X. The critical primes are exactly those attaining the minimum: the active constraints of the construction, analogous to tight rows in an optimal linear program. Every other prime has slack.

At X = 10⁴, seven primes are critical. The count belongs to the certified set, not merely to its density: at X = 10³, two different sets attain the same record 2/29 but have different critical sets, of sizes 8 and 6.

§5Conductors

A modulus sweep contains large exact plateaus. If the record at m0 is {n : cn mod m0 < L}, then at every multiple k m0 the pair (kc, kL) defines literally the same periodic set. The same density therefore reappears automatically at all multiples of the original modulus.

Borrowing terminology from Dirichlet characters, call these larger representations induced, and call the smallest modulus carrying the structure its conductor. The mathematically interesting objects in a sweep are the primitive conductors, not their repeated multiples.

The X = 10⁴ record is especially rigid: 3848 = 2³·13·37, and every proper divisor is dead. It survives by exactly one power of two: requiring 4 | p−1 raises the kill condition to p ≡ 1 (mod 1924), whose least prime is 11545 > 10⁴.

§6The three-point ladder

Now consider the length-three progression x, x + (p−1), x + (p−1)², the model configuration of the Krause–Mousavi–Tao–Teräväinen paper.

Write δ₂(X) for the best two-point density found and δ₃(X) for the corresponding three-point density. Avoiding the whole three-point pattern is a weaker requirement than avoiding either pair separately, so δ₃ ≥ δ₂. The computation shows considerably more than that: the ratio grows rapidly with X.

For an interval set, put d = p−1. A pattern occurs exactly when the three residues {0, cd, cd²} mod m fit inside a cyclic window of L residues. Equivalently, the margin is the circular span m − maxgap(0, cd, cd²). The prime p = 2 is degenerate because d = d², and is skipped.

There is also an important arithmetic difference from the two-point case. Blocking now fails as soon as m | d. Thus a modulus is alive at threshold X exactly when the least prime p ≡ 1 (mod m) exceeds X. The winning moduli at X = 10⁴ through 10⁶ have least such primes at roughly 13, 24, 17, 38, and 32 times their moduli.

The initial three-point law.

For seven successive thresholds, the normalized prefactor associated with an X−0.267 fit stays in the narrow band [1.06, 1.20]. At X = 10⁵ it falls to 0.96, at 3·10⁵ to 0.90, and at 10⁶ to 0.86.

The first two declines occur well inside the searched ranges: the corresponding sweep limits are about 23 and 14 times the winning moduli. The 10⁶ rung is the exception so far, searched only to 1.1 times its winning modulus, with a wider sweep in progress. The mod-4 factorization pattern of the two-point conductors is absent here: conductors such as 211, 353, and 2·11·13·23 mix both odd residue classes modulo 4. That regularity therefore appears specific to the pure-square configuration.

§7What the bends seem to mean

Both searches have now been extended well beyond their winning moduli, so the main question has shifted from whether the bends are real to why they occur.

For the three-point problem, a sieve-only probe separates two regimes. At small X, the winners sit far above the arithmetic floor of the alive-modulus pool and geometry dominates. From X = 10⁴ onward, however, the winner's φ(m) collapses onto the minimum φ among all alive moduli: the ratios are 58–120 times the floor at X = 100–1000, then 1.3, 1.0, 1.1, and 1.0 across X = 10⁴ to 3·10⁵.

That much identifies the three-point bend with pool depletion: exceptional viable moduli disappear faster than new ones become available. The next rung qualifies the picture. At X = 10⁶ the pinning breaks: the record sits at m = 54386, whose φ(m) is about 1.66 times the floor, and it beat the floor modulus on geometry even though that modulus lay inside the searched range. The floor drives the decline without fixing the records to it.

The two-point problem does not pin in the same way. Its winning conductors remain about 1.5–5 times above the corresponding floor, with no downward trend; at the bend the ratio is still about 3.1. The two ladders therefore share a visible signature, but apparently not a common mechanism.

The sieve-only floor, computed by pool_floor.py, extends beyond the measured ladder and gives falsifiable predictions

δ₃(10⁶) ≈ 0.0170,  δ₃(3·10⁶) ≈ 0.0114,  δ₃(10⁷) ≈ 0.0073.

The first of these has now been tested. The measured record 1160/54386 ≈ 0.0213 came in about 25% above it, so the floor is better read as a lower anchor than as a law; the remaining two values stand as anchors of the same kind.

The local growth of the floor, with slopes around 0.7–1.0, suggests that the observed 0.267 law was a transient of a rich modulus pool. On this evidence, the eventual three-point behavior is tied to the large-deviation statistics of least primes in arithmetic progressions.

Several questions remain open:

  • Why do all observed two-point conductors, apart from powers of two, factor entirely into primes ≡ 1 (mod 4)?
  • What causes the two-point bend if it is not depletion of the conductor pool?
  • Does restricting square differences to (p−1)² ultimately improve the asymptotic density over unrestricted square-difference avoidance?
  • Do exact independence numbers beyond periods of roughly 80 reveal denser non-interval constructions?
  • Can the small critical-prime sets be used systematically to construct the next record?

§8Certification and reproducibility

The computational claims on this page are designed to be independently checkable. The search harness stores a finite set A ⊆ [0, N) as a bitmask. For each prime, the relevant translates are combined by shift-and-AND operations. A surviving bit gives an explicit witness; a zero intersection is an exhaustive proof of absence throughout the searched window.

For periodic Cantor–Gordon constructions, the decisive check can instead be made exactly modulo the period. Each certificate records the construction parameters, per-prime results and cryptographic digests of the mathematically relevant data. The core digest is stable across schema changes.

For example:

python shifted_prime_patterns.py --verify certificates/cgX10000.json

A passing run rebuilds the archived result from its recorded parameters and matches the stored mask and core digests. The expandable tables above retain the prime counts, critical-prime counts, search limits and certificate identifiers without making them part of the main reading path.

All data and certificates are available at github.com/michaelmross/shifted-prime-patterns.
